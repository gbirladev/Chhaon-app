A simple BP and diabetes medicine timetable for parents (free printable)

By Team Chhaon · 8 November 2026 · हिंदी में पढ़ें

Blood pressure and sugar medicines are often taken for years, sometimes several at different times of day. For a parent at home, and for you checking from another city, a simple printed timetable on the wall removes a lot of confusion. This guide shows how to make one that is easy to read and easy to keep up to date.

A timetable does not decide anything about treatment. It only writes down, clearly, what the doctor has already prescribed.

Why a printed timetable helps

  • Everyone at home reads the same thing: your parent, the other parent, a helper, a visiting relative.
  • It shows at a glance which tablets go in the morning, afternoon and night.
  • After a doctor visit, you can see exactly what changed.
  • In an emergency, it is a quick, readable list to hand over.

The World Health Organization notes that diabetes is managed with diet, physical activity and, for many people, medicines, and that people with diabetes often also need medicines for blood pressure. That's why many parents have a longer list than we expect, and why writing it down clearly matters.

What to put on it

Take the latest prescription and copy, for each medicine:

  1. Name, exactly as written on the prescription or strip. Brand and generic names can look different; if unsure, ask the pharmacist.
  2. What it's for, in simple words, only if the doctor told you (for example "BP" or "sugar").
  3. When: morning, afternoon, night, as the doctor said.
  4. Food: before or after food, only as the doctor or the label says.
  5. Notes: anything the doctor asked, like "with water" or "check sugar first".

Do not copy amounts or timings from someone else's prescription, the internet, or memory. If something on the prescription is unclear, call the doctor's clinic or ask the pharmacist.

Make it easy to read

  • Big text. Use a large font and leave space between rows. Bold the time headings.
  • Their language. If your parent reads Hindi more easily, make the timetable in Hindi.
  • Match the strips. Write the name as it appears on the strip they actually have at home. If you can, stick a small cut-out of the strip label next to the name.
  • Date it. Write "Updated on: ____" at the top, and the doctor's name.
  • One sheet only. Throw away old copies, so there's never confusion about which is current.

Where to keep it

Stick it where the medicines are kept, at eye level, and keep a photo on your phone and with your siblings. A copy in the bag for doctor visits is useful too: the doctor can see the full list at once.

If the doctor asked for home readings

Some doctors ask families to check BP or sugar at home. If yours did, keep a simple log next to the timetable: date, time, reading, and a note if your parent felt unwell. Take it to the next visit. Don't change any medicine because of a reading; if a reading worries you, call the doctor.

Keep it current

A timetable is only useful if it matches today's prescription. Every time a doctor adds, stops or changes a medicine, update the sheet the same day and reprint it. It also helps to ask the doctor at each visit: "Can you check this list is still correct?"

Checklist for your timetable

  • Made from the latest prescription, not from memory.
  • Every current medicine listed, with names that match the strips at home.
  • Morning / afternoon / night and before / after food, only as the doctor said.
  • Large print, in the language your parent reads best.
  • "Updated on" date and the doctor's name at the top.
  • Stuck near the medicines; photo on your phone; old copies thrown away.
  • Doctor's and pharmacist's numbers written at the bottom, for questions about missed doses.

This is not medical advice. Please talk to your doctor. If a dose is missed, ask the doctor or pharmacist what to do; don't guess.
