How to make sure your elderly parents take their medicines on time (when you live in another city)

By Team Chhaon · 8 November 2026 · हिंदी में पढ़ें

You call Mummy in the evening and ask, "Did Papa take his tablets?" She says yes. But neither of you is fully sure about the afternoon one. If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Many of us live in Bengaluru, Pune, Delhi or Hyderabad while our parents stay in the hometown, and medicines are one of the things we worry about most.

The good news: you don't need to be in the same house to help. A few simple systems, set up once and checked gently, make a real difference. Here is what works for many families.

Why doses get missed (it's rarely carelessness)

Before fixing anything, it helps to understand why it happens. Parents usually want to take their medicines. Doses slip because of ordinary things:

  • Several tablets at different times of day, and some that changed at the last doctor visit.
  • Strips that look alike, small print, or weaker eyesight.
  • A busy morning, a guest at home, a wedding, a train journey.
  • Feeling fine, so the tablet "can wait till later", and later is forgotten.
  • The strip running out, and nobody going to the chemist in time.

When you see it this way, the conversation stops being about blame and starts being about making things easier.

Step 1: Make one clear, current list

Start with the latest prescription, not memory. Write down every medicine your parent takes now: the name as written on the prescription, when it is taken (morning, afternoon, night), whether the doctor said before or after food, and which doctor prescribed it.

Keep a photo of this list and of the prescription on your phone, and a printed copy at home where the medicines are kept. If anything is unclear, or two doctors have prescribed different things, ask the doctor or pharmacist to go through the list with you. Do not fill gaps on your own.

The World Health Organization's advice on high blood pressure is simple: take medicines as prescribed by your health care professional, and don't miss or share them. A clear list is the first step to doing that.

Step 2: Tie medicines to the daily routine

Habits stick when they are attached to something that already happens every day: morning tea, the afternoon meal, the evening news, the night prayer. Within the timings the doctor gave, pick a fixed moment for each dose and keep it the same every day.

Keep the medicines in one fixed, visible place near that moment, for example on the dining table shelf, not in three different drawers.

Step 3: Make the tablets easy to see

A simple weekly pill organiser (the plastic box with Morning / Night sections for each day) is one of the most useful things you can buy. Someone fills it once a week, and at a glance anyone can see whether today's dose has been taken.

  • Choose who fills it: a parent, a sibling nearby, or a trusted helper. You can video-call while it's being filled.
  • Use big, clear labels. Write in the language your parent reads most easily.
  • Ask the pharmacist if any medicine should not be taken out of its strip early.

Step 4: Pick a reminder your parent is comfortable with

There is no single best reminder. The right one is the one your parent actually notices and doesn't find annoying:

  • A phone alarm with a label like "BP tablet, after breakfast". Simple, but easy to switch off and forget.
  • A call at a fixed time from you or a sibling. Warm and personal, but hard to keep up every day around office hours.
  • A helper at home who hands over the medicines. Very reliable when available; agree clearly on what they do and do not do.
  • A WhatsApp message at dose time. Most parents already use WhatsApp, and a one-tap reply tells you the dose was taken.

Many families combine two: a pill organiser plus a reminder that someone in the family can see.

Step 5: Never let the strip run out

A surprising number of missed doses are simply "the medicine was finished". Count how many tablets are left of each medicine, and plan the refill at least a week before it runs out. Keep a copy of the prescription with the local chemist or on your phone, and check if they deliver home. Ask the doctor well before a prescription needs renewing.

Step 6: Check in without nagging

Parents are adults who raised us. Being asked "Did you take it?" five times a day can feel like being treated as a child. Some things that help:

  • Agree on the plan together, and ask what would make it easier for them.
  • Check quietly (a reply on a message, a glance at the organiser on a video call) instead of questioning.
  • Thank them, not just check them. "Good, Papa, all done for today" goes a long way.
  • If a dose is missed, stay calm. Ask the doctor or pharmacist what to do; don't guess, and don't tell them to make up for it on your own.

Quick checklist

  • One current medicine list, made from the latest prescription, photo on your phone.
  • Each dose tied to a fixed daily moment, within the doctor's timings.
  • A weekly pill organiser, and a person who fills it.
  • A reminder your parent is comfortable with, and someone who sees the reply.
  • Tablet count and a refill date a week before stock runs out.
  • The doctor's and chemist's numbers saved, for questions about missed doses.
  • A friendly check-in, not a daily interrogation.
